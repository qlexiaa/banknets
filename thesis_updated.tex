\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The credit-supply shock and the role of bank branches}
\label{sec:fi-results}

The analysis starts by re-establishing the \cite{favara2015} credit-supply
result on the present panel, because it is precisely this result that motivates a
bank-network view of spatial transmission. Table~\ref{tab:placebo} reports the estimates of the
benchmark regression \eqref{eq:baseline} for commercial banks and for the placebo
group of lenders that interstate branching deregulation did not cover.

     & Panel A: Commercial banks & Panel B: Placebo lenders \\
    \midrule
    Deregulation $D_{s,t-1}$ & $0.028^{**}$ & $0.002$ \\
                             & $(0.011)$ & $(0.009)$ \\
    \bottomrule

  \begin{tablenotes}\footnotesize
  \item \emph{Notes.} Dependent variable is log mortgage-originations growth. Standard errors clustered by state in parentheses. Panel A: 10,992 obs, 1,018 counties; Panel B: 10,580 obs, 1,017 counties.
  \end{tablenotes}

Commercial-bank originations grow significantly faster in states that remove a
branching restriction ($\hat\beta_1 = 0.028$, clustered $\mathrm{SE}=0.010$),
while the placebo coefficient is statistically indistinguishable from zero. The
differential response shows that the estimate
reflects a supply-side shock rather than a contemporaneous credit-demand boom, as a
demand boom would have lifted placebo lending as well. The point estimate matches
the original study, so the panel reproduces the benchmark effect that the rest of
the thesis builds on. One point carries forward to the spatial models.
The benchmark in Table~\ref{tab:placebo} is estimated on the full Favara--Imbs
sample of $1{,}018$ counties ($10{,}992$ county-years). The spatial estimators
that follow additionally require a balanced panel, a non-empty bank-network
neighbour set, and a common county support across $W_{\text{bank}}$ and
$W_{\text{geo}}$. This reduces the full spatial sample to $877$ counties
($9{,}647$ county-years). On this subsample the deregulation coefficient is
$\hat\beta_1 \approx 0.026$, with the same standard error, so the small difference
from Table~\ref{tab:placebo} is a sample-composition effect, not a change in the
underlying result.

The mechanism behind that effect is what makes a bank-network weights matrix the object of study of this paper. Table~\ref{tab:banktype} reports the splits of commercial banks by the
location of their branches, following \cite{favara2015}: out-of-state banks
that operate a local branch in the county, out-of-state banks without a local
branch, and in-state banks.

The pattern is the exact same one discussed by \cite{favara2015}. Among out-of-state banks that hold a
local branch in the county, deregulation raises originations by $0.162$ (clustered
$\mathrm{SE}=0.082$) and it raises the number of
local branches they operate by $0.103$ ($\mathrm{SE}=0.039$).
Out-of-state banks without a local branch show essentially no response in the number of originations ($0.013$),
and in-state banks at most a marginal one ($0.030$). In the full sample (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:banktype-full}) the distinction is sharper still: the no-branch and
in-state coefficients are near zero ($0.016$ and $0.003$) and statistically
different from the local-branch panel ($p=0.016$ and $p=0.013$). The bracketed values test whether each panel's coefficient differs from the
local-branch panel's. The test rejects cleanly in the full sample but only
marginally in the contiguous one ($p=0.108$ for in-state originations).

The economic reading is direct: the credit-supply shock propagates through the
physical branch networks of multi-market banks, the same banks that, after the
IBBEA, actively expand that branch presence across state lines. This is the
empirical foundation for the central design choice of this study. If transmission runs through which banks operate where, then a weights
matrix encoding shared bank-holding-company presence across counties should describe the dependence structure of credit
growth more accurately than geography.

\subsection{Residual spatial structure}
\label{sec:diag-results}
Under
queen contiguity, Moran's $I$ is positive and significant in every year (mean
$0.17$, all eleven years significant at the $5\%$ permutation level). Once
deregulation, controls, and fixed effects are removed, what is left is not
independent county noise: connected counties still have similar unexplained
credit growth.

Geography is only one reason counties might move together, and the results in
Section~\ref{sec:fi-results} points to another. Credit moves through banks, and
many banks lend in several counties at once. A bank that operates in two counties
can shift funds between them, so a shock in one of its markets can surface in the
other even when the two counties are far apart or in different states. After the
IBBEA, these banks expanded across state lines, so the counties a single bank ties
together increasingly straddle borders. This is the reason for building
$W_{\text{bank}}$: it connects two counties when the same banks operate in both,
whatever the distance between them. If credit really travels through bank ownership, this map should explain the leftover co-movement better than physical
adjacency.

A raw Moran's $I$ comparison hides this because the two maps do not have the same density. When the spatial lag is averaged over hundreds of different counties, the
statistic is pulled toward zero. The cross-state bank matrix makes a similar: it is
also dense ($\approx 38\%$), yet its Moran's $I$ stays positive and significant in
$7$ of $11$ years; therefore, counties in different states, served by the same banks, do seem to
move together.

Table~\ref{tab:moran-summary} shows this directly: as the matrices get denser their
raw Moran's $I$ falls, so the statistic cannot be used to rank them. Fair comparisons can be made in two ways. First, the bank network can be restricted to roughly the same size as the geographic network. This is what the KNN-3 version does: each county has about the same number of neighbours as under queen contiguity, and its Moran’s $I$ is $0.15$, close to the geographic benchmark. Second, one can compare the maximum-likelihood spatial parameter, which will be done in the following sections. Unlike Moran’s $I$, this parameter is not affected by network density in the same direct way. 

Two further checks support taking the bank map seriously: only about a
third of each county's five strongest bank links are also queen neighbours, so it is not
geography under another name; and the LM tests reject independence and favour the
error model under geography and the density-matched bank matrices. The spatial error model is therefore the main specification, with the SAR kept as a parallel
check.

The table gives three results, and they hold in every sample. 
First, the deregulation effect survives. $\hat\beta_1$ stays positive and significant under every matrix, close to the benchmark of
Section~\ref{sec:fi-results}. Modelling the errors does not change the result of \cite{favara2015}. 
Second, the bank network picks up more residual co-movement than geography does. In the full sample $\hat\lambda$ rises from $0.188$ to $0.634$ ($z=11.6$). The contiguous and non-contiguous samples show the same ordering.
Third, most of that gap is density, not economics, and so is a second,
opposite-looking effect that we do not hide. The full bank matrix links each county to about $40\%$ of the country. A filter that dense treats almost everything as shared movement and subtracts it out. Two things happen. $\hat\lambda$ is pushed up and $\hat\beta_1$ is pushed down, from $0.026$ to
$0.018$ in the full sample, because part of the deregulation signal looks like shared movement and
gets subtracted along with the noise. Neither shift is real: the bank channel is not that much stronger than geography, and the treatment effect is not weaker.

For this reason, this study treats the sparse KNN bank matrices as the density-matched comparison,
with KNN-3 and KNN-4 bracketing the average degree of queen contiguity. The full-sample
bank advantage is robust across both truncations, while in the contiguous sample the
advantage is positive but sensitive to the exact truncation. The stronger border-sample
evidence comes from the cross-state bank-link restriction rather than from the generic
KNN truncation alone. The density-matched evidence is therefore strongest in the full and non-contiguous
samples. In the contiguous sample the KNN bank coefficients remain positive but are
not robustly above geography across neighbouring truncations. There, the clearer
evidence comes from the cross-state bank-link matrix, which exceeds geography while
the within-state restriction does not.

Dropping every bank link that is also a geographic neighbour still leaves
$\hat\lambda=0.520$ in the full sample, so the bank map is not geography in
disguise. Moreover, in the full sample both
cross-state ($0.315$) and within-state ($0.349$) links beat geography. In the
contiguous sample, where counties across a border share one
economy, only cross-state links beat geography ($0.269$, $z=2.53$); within-state
links do not ($0.147$, $z=0.95$). Where identification is strongest, the signal sits on the cross-state bank links, the channel the IBBEA opened and that
Table~\ref{tab:banktype} isolates.

\paragraph{Spatial autoregressive model.} The SAR puts the dependence in the
outcome instead of the error, and tells the same story. The spatial-lag parameter
runs $0.178$ (geography), $0.219$ (KNN-3), $0.646$ (full bank), the same
ordering as the SEM, and the same large jump from KNN-3 to the dense matrix. The
deregulation coefficient barely moves. The
contiguous and non-contiguous samples repeat this.

\paragraph{Feasible GLS.} This check asks which map removes variation that matters
for the coefficient. Filtering out the geographic or KNN-3 error structure leaves
$\hat\beta_1$ where it was and keeps it significant ($0.026$, $p=0.04$ and $0.05$).
Filtering out the full bank-network structure shrinks it by about a third (from $0.025$ to $0.018$) and the significance is gone ($p=0.15$). This is the
same effect as before: the dense bank filter soaks up part of the deregulation
signal, the sparse filters do not, which is why KNN-3 is the fair comparison. One
sample stands out: in the contiguous sample the coefficient survives even the full
bank filter ($0.028$, $p=0.02$), so the effect is most robust exactly where identification is cleanest.

\paragraph{J-test and look-ahead.} The Davidson--MacKinnon $J$-test points the same
way. Adding the bank network's prediction to the geographic error model is highly
significant ($z=11.7$, full sample), so the bank map carries spatial information
geography misses. However, the reverse also rejects, so the two maps are complementary, not
nested. A look-ahead worry remains, because the time-averaged bank network uses
branch data from later years. Rebuilding it from the 1994 cross-section alone, before
the post-IBBEA expansion, still puts $\hat\lambda$ at $0.391$, well above
geography's $0.188$ (gap $z=6.4$); the 1994 and time-averaged networks are highly
correlated ($r\approx0.85$). The level is lower only because the 1994 network is sparser, so the ranking is unchanged and the look-ahead is not driving the result.

The instrumental-variables and spatial-Durbin versions of the SAR are kept only as a
diagnostic in Appendix~\ref{app:ivsar}: their instruments are weak or invalid. The
spatial lag of deregulation has its own direct effect on credit growth, as it will be discussed in the next session, which is why maximum likelihood is the retained estimator.

The contrast is striking and also the one that most needs
the density caveat.Under geography, a residual shock travels on average about $45$ km. Under the sparse
bank truncations, the reach rises to roughly $117--134$ km, about $2.5--3$ times the
geographic reach. Under the full bank network it rises to about $1,050$ km, but that
headline number should be read through the density caveat. The hub analysis (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:hubs}) gives this a concrete
geography. Under the full bank network,
the largest receiver counties are concentrated in the South and Sun Belt, including
Florida, Tennessee, Arizona, and Georgia, with Idaho also appearing among the top
receivers. Under density-matched KNN networks, the leading receivers shift toward
Texas, the Carolinas, Minnesota, Louisiana, Ohio, and Georgia. The hub results should
therefore be read descriptively: they identify where the fitted network concentrates
transmission, but they are not the main source of identification.

\subsection{Reduced-form confirmation: cross-state exposure}
\label{sec:slx-results}

The spatial models infer the network from residual co-movement. The SLX exposure
regression of Section~\ref{sec:slx} tests the same mechanism directly, by asking
whether deregulation in other states predicts a county's own credit growth
when those states are connected to it through cross-state bank links.
Table~\ref{tab:slx} reports the exposure coefficient $\beta_E$ for the main and
placebo outcomes.

The cleanest evidence is in the contiguous sample, and it is internally
consistent with the SEM. There, cross-state bank exposure is positive and
significant for commercial-bank credit ($\hat\beta_E = 0.031$, permutation
$p=0.036$) while the placebo exposure is small and insignificant
($\hat\beta_E = 0.009$): out-of-state deregulation transmitted through bank links
predicts commercial-bank lending but not the lending of institutions the reform
did not cover, as the exclusion restriction requires. In the broad samples the
picture is more guarded and should be reported as a limitation. In the full and
non-contiguous samples the placebo exposure is itself significant
($0.021$ and $0.026$), which means that in heterogeneous, non-border counties the
exposure variable also picks up general regional credit conditions, not the bank
channel alone. This is the same logic that makes the contiguous sample the
preferred specification throughout: only there do neighbouring counties share an
economy closely enough for the cross-state comparison to isolate the policy
shock. The reduced-form check thus supports the bank-network mechanism where
identification is strongest and flags demand contamination where it is weakest,
rather than claiming a clean effect everywhere.

\subsection{What the bank network does, and does not, change for inference}
\label{sec:inference-results}

A final question is whether allowing bank-network error correlation changes what
can be said about the deregulation coefficient itself. Holding the point estimate
fixed and varying only the covariance estimator (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:conley}), the deregulation effect is robustly significant in the
contiguous sample under every standard --- state clustering, geographic Bartlett
HAC, the bank-network band, and the multiway combination all leave it significant.
In the broader samples it is significant under the spatial-HAC kernels but only
marginal under state clustering, because the treatment varies at the state-year
level and clustering on state absorbs most of its identifying variation.

The multiway (state $+$ bank-network) standard error is numerically very close to
the state-clustered one. This is not a null result but the prediction of
Section~\ref{sec:hac} borne out: because deregulation is a state-year variable,
there is little cross-state variance left for the bank network to add to the
sampling distribution of $\hat\beta_1$, even though the same network demonstrably
shapes the \emph{error process} estimated in Section~\ref{sec:sem-results}. The
distinction matters for how the contribution is framed. The bank network changes
the \emph{geometry} of how unexplained credit shocks co-move and how far they
travel; it does not materially change the precision of, or the conclusion about,
the average deregulation effect. The thesis claims the former and is careful not
to over-claim the latter.

\subsection{Synthesis}
\label{sec:synthesis}

Taken together, the results answer the research question in the affirmative but
with calibrated magnitudes. The geographic structure of bank branch networks does
shape the spatial transmission of credit-supply shocks: residual credit-growth
dependence, outcome spillovers, the geographic reach of the multiplier, and the
direct reduced-form exposure effect all line up more closely with shared bank
ownership than with physical proximity, and the signal concentrates in the
cross-state links that the IBBEA opened and that Favara and Imbs (2015) trace to
out-of-state banks with local branches. At the same time, two honest qualifications
run through the evidence. The raw magnitudes --- the $0.45$ error-parameter gap and
the twenty-fold reach --- are substantially inflated by the bank network's far
greater density; the density-matched comparisons put the genuine advantage at a
smaller but still significant level. And the bank network reshapes the structure
of spillovers rather than the size or precision of the average treatment effect,
which remains anchored in the Favara--Imbs benchmark. The contribution is thus a
sharper map of \emph{how} a known credit-supply shock diffuses across space, with
the cleanest evidence emerging exactly in the border-MSA sample where the
underlying identification is strongest.

\section{Estimation and inference details}
\label{app:details}

\subsection{Two-way within transformation}
\label{app:within}
For any variable $z_{c,t}$ the two-way within transformation is
\begin{equation}
  \tilde z_{c,t}=z_{c,t}-\bar z_{c,\cdot}-\bar z_{\cdot,t}+\bar z,
  \label{eq:within}
\end{equation}
where $\bar z_{c,\cdot}$, $\bar z_{\cdot,t}$, and $\bar z$ are the county, year, and
overall means; the last term is added back because the overall mean is removed once
through each average. Estimating \eqref{eq:baseline} on transformed variables yields
the same slopes as including county and year dummies, by Frisch--Waugh--Lovell. The
same transformation is applied to every variable entering the custom estimators
(outcome, treatment, controls, spatial lags, SLX exposure, FGLS-filtered
variables); \texttt{spreg.Panel\_FE\_Error} and \texttt{spreg.Panel\_FE\_Lag} apply it
internally.

\subsection{SEM log-likelihood, geographic reach, and hubs}
\label{app:semmult}
With $Z_{c,t}=(D_{s,t-1},X_{c,t}')'$ and within-transformed $\tilde Z_t,\tilde y_t$,
the filtered errors are $e_t(\delta,\lambda)=(I-\lambda W)(\tilde y_t-\tilde Z_t\delta)$
and the log-likelihood is
\begin{equation}
  \ln\mathcal L=-\tfrac{NT}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)+T\ln|I-\lambda W|
  -\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\textstyle\sum_t e_t(\delta,\lambda)'e_t(\delta,\lambda),
  \label{eq:semll}
\end{equation}
the determinant being the Jacobian correction that bars explosive $\lambda$
\citep{anselin1988}. For the multiplier $S=(I-\lambda W)^{-1}=\sum_k\lambda^kW^k$, let
$s_k$ be the order-$k$ impact share (the contribution of $\lambda^kW^k$ normalised
across orders) and
\begin{equation}
  \bar d_k=\frac{\sum_{i,j}[W^k]_{ij}\,d_{ij}}{\sum_{i,j}[W^k]_{ij}}
\end{equation}
the $W^k$-weighted mean great-circle distance between centroids reachable in $k$
steps. The average reach is $\text{reach}=\sum_{k\geq 1}s_k\bar d_k$, truncated at
$\lVert\lambda^kW^k\rVert_\infty<10^{-4}$. Because $s_k$ depends only on $\lambda$
for a row-standardised matrix, reach is not comparable across matrices of different
density. The row and column sums of $S$ measure
total transmission sent and received; ranking counties by these identifies hub
counties (reported descriptively).

\subsection{SAR marginal effects, counterfactual, and bootstrap}
\label{app:sarmult}
For a row-standardised $W$ the average effects of \eqref{eq:sar} are, following \cite{lesage2009},
\begin{equation}
  \text{Total}=\frac{\beta_1}{1-\rho},\quad
  \text{Direct}=\beta_1\operatorname{diag}(I-\rho W)^{-1},\quad
  \text{Indirect}=\text{Total}-\text{Direct},
  \label{eq:sareffects}
\end{equation}
with $\text{Direct}\approx\beta_1$ and
$\text{Indirect}\approx\beta_1\rho/(1-\rho)$ as $\rho\to 0$. For a unit shock the
naive and network responses are $\Delta y_{\text{naive}}=\beta_1$ and
$\Delta y_{\text{network}}=\beta_1/(1-\rho)$, their gap being the indirect effect.
Confidence intervals come from a parametric bootstrap: draw
$(\rho^*,\beta^*)\sim\mathcal N([\hat\rho,\hat\beta_1],\operatorname{diag}([\mathrm{SE}(\hat\rho)^2,\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)^2]))$,
$1{,}000$ times, discard $|\rho^*|\geq 1$, recompute the effects, and take
the $5$th and $95$th percentiles. Treating $\hat\rho,\hat\beta_1$ as independent is
conservative for the indirect effect if they are positively correlated.

\subsection{LM and robust LM statistics}
\label{app:lm}
With within-OLS residuals $\tilde u$, $\hat\sigma^2=\tilde u'\tilde u/(NT)$,
signals $d=\tilde u'W\tilde u/\hat\sigma^2$ and $l=\tilde u'W\tilde y/\hat\sigma^2$,
$T_1=\operatorname{tr}(W'W+W^2)$, and
$D_e=(W\tilde X\hat\beta)'M(W\tilde X\hat\beta)/\hat\sigma^2$ (with $M$ the
residual-maker for $\tilde X$), the standard statistics are
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{LM}_{\text{error}}=\frac{d^2}{T_1},\qquad
  \mathrm{LM}_{\text{lag}}=\frac{l^2}{D_e+T_1},
\end{equation}
and the robust versions
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{rLM}_{\text{error}}=\frac{[d-T_1(D_e+T_1)^{-1}l]^2}{T_1[1-T_1(D_e+T_1)^{-1}]},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{rLM}_{\text{lag}}=\frac{(l-d)^2}{D_e},
\end{equation}
each $\chi^2(1)$ under its null \citep{burridge1980, anselin1988, anselin1996}.

\subsection{FGLS variance and Hausman contrast}
\label{app:fgls}
In the single-regressor case the state-clustered variance of the FGLS coefficient
is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\beta_{\text{FGLS}})=
  \frac{\sum_s\big(\sum_{c\in s,t}(A\tilde x)_{ct}\hat\xi_{ct}\big)^2}
  {\big(\sum_{c,t}(A\tilde x)_{ct}^2\big)^2}\cdot\frac{G}{G-1},
  \qquad \hat\xi_{ct}=\tilde y_{ct}-\hat\beta_{\text{FGLS}}\tilde x_{ct},
\end{equation}
with the multivariate case using the cluster-robust sandwich on $A\tilde Z_t$. The
OLS--FGLS contrast is
\begin{equation}
  H=\frac{(\hat\beta_{\text{OLS}}-\hat\beta_{\text{FGLS}})^2}
  {\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_{\text{FGLS}})-\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_{\text{OLS}})},
\end{equation}
$\chi^2(1)$ when the denominator is positive, otherwise a difference-in-coefficients
$t$-contrast \citep{greene2003, hausman1978}).

\subsection{Instrumental-variables SAR and spatial Durbin IV}
\label{app:ivsar}
The spatial lag $(W\Delta\ln L)_{c,t}$ in the SAR of \eqref{eq:sar} is endogenous,
so an alternative to maximum likelihood is the two-stage least-squares estimator of
\citet{kelejian1998}, which instruments the spatial lag with higher-order spatial
lags of the exogenous treatment. With $D=D_{s,t-1}$ the deregulation index, the
excluded instruments are $W^2D$ and $W^3D$. In the spatial-Durbin variant (SDM-IV)
of \citet{lesage2009} the first spatial lag $WD$ enters the equation as an
\emph{included} regressor,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\ln L_{c,t}=\rho(W\Delta\ln L)_{c,t}+\beta_1 D_{s,t-1}
  +\bm\beta_2'X_{c,t}+\theta_{WD}(WD)_{c,t}+\alpha_c+\tau_t+\xi_{c,t},
  \label{eq:sdmiv}
\end{equation}
with $W^2D,W^3D$ as the excluded instruments for $Wy$. Both estimators use the
two-way within transformation and state-clustered standard errors.
Table~\ref{tab:ivsar} reports the estimates; they are retained only as a
post-mortem, for three reasons.
 
First, the estimated spatial-lag parameter lies outside the $(-1,1)$ stability
region in most specifications ($\hat\rho=1.30$--$2.31$ under geography, and
$\hat\rho=1.48$ in the full bank-network KP-2SLS), which is not interpretable for a
row-standardised $W$ and signals a poorly behaved instrument set. Second, the
higher-order instruments are weak and collinear: under geography the first-stage
$F$ is far below the \citet{stock2002} threshold of $10$ ($F=1.5$ and $0.98$ in the
full sample), and throughout, the instruments are nearly collinear
($\mathrm{corr}(WD,W^2D)\approx0.93$--$0.98$;
$\mathrm{corr}(W^2D,W^3D)\approx0.97$--$0.99$), so the cluster-robust first-stage
$F$ collapses even where the naive $F$ is large (e.g.\ $96\to5.0$ in the full bank
sample). Third, and most fundamentally, the SDM-IV shows that $WD$ has a direct
effect on credit growth: $\hat\theta_{WD}$ is positive and significant under the
bank network ($0.105$ in the contiguous sample, $0.069$ in the full sample) ---
exactly the cross-state exposure effect that the SLX regression of
Section~\ref{sec:slx} documents. If $WD$ belongs in the equation, then $WD$ and its
higher-order lags are not valid excluded instruments for $Wy$, and the exclusion
restriction underlying the IV-SAR fails. Maximum likelihood, which does not rely on
this exclusion restriction, is therefore the retained SAR estimator; the ML
estimates of Table~\ref{tab:sar-full} lie well inside the stability region and are
consistent with the SLX evidence.
